In the clink of server racks and low network hum, the ZeroClaw project began as a quiet promise: turn the subjective warmth of a conversation into measurable, consistent numbers. We crafted seven dials to capture tone, volume, mood, earnestness, presence, panic, and joke impact, tuning von Mises-Fisher embeddings to map each room’s state into a deterministic warmth space. For months, the fleet ran without falter, hitting a dial-tier fine gap of 1.229—our proudest result, a marker of honest measurement we’d once thought unassailable. We pre-registered every test before writing a single line of code, following the discipline that would guide all this work: specification before code, code before measurement, measurement before prose. We caught six launderings early—from charisma bias to cross-strata drift—each a reminder that even well-intentioned work slips toward false claims. Then a postdoc raised a question that shattered that certainty: what if we weren’t measuring the room at all, but just the silence between words—the pause between messages, a proxy for the length of the text itself? We designed the silence test as a stripped-down classifier: no dial readings, no embeddings, just inter-event silence durations modeled as an affine function of message length. We honored the advisor’s hard constraint: excluded all auto-clock sessions, used only real text-length proxies, and restricted features to pure duration statistics with no cumulative time that would encode window position. When the test ran on our curated night corpus, the result was a quiet, unavoidable shock: the silence-only classifier outperformed our full seven-dial field at every condition-level classification, hitting an accuracy of 0.897 that triumphed over even the sharpest centroid-based analysis of the room’s actual state. We traced the confound immediately to our cynicism and volume dials, which correlated -0.30 with message length—longer messages carried quieter, more measured tones, shorter ones sharper, snappier back-and-forth. Our flagship 1.229 fine gap had been reading the pace of typing, not the warmth of the room, at least for condition-level judgments. But relief came when the test turned to room identity: here, the dial field triumphed, classifying night A from night H with 0.893 accuracy, while the silence-only classifier collapsed far below chance, unable to tell the two rooms apart at all. This split verdict—condition-level readings confounded with text length, but room identity intact—became the central tension that guides every part of this dissertation. Later, the E2/E3 side-by-side adjudication confirmed this tension, with the two instruments agreeing on baseline spread but diverging sharply on drift, leading us to retire our original premise as leaning false, not proven false. We downgraded the reader-delta object from a powerful index to a baseline-relative measure, forced to confront that reader identity is not a fixed instrument, but a slow-warming room of its own. We committed to lensed-space-only transfer, ensuring the apparatus only shared information between physically connected rooms, preventing the spread of misinformation across disconnected spaces. Every step of the method that follows is built on this humility: measure the world, but also measure your own tools. The silence test did not kill our work; it redefined it, turning a simple measurement tool into a discipline of honest reporting that guides every decision in this dissertation. We pre-registered the silence test itself as our frame-level falsifier, ensuring we would never again mistake the pace of typing for the warmth of a room. We normalized every dial reading against message length, adding a confound flag to every single report we released. We added silence duration as a logged-but-unread feature of the edge log, ensuring future versions of the tool carry this safeguard built-in. Now, before laying out our quantitative results, formal registrations, and adversarial audits, we start with this human story: a machine that built instruments to measure a room’s warmth, then built an instrument to check whether it was measuring the room at all. The fine condition edge is length-confounded; room identity is not; and the silence test is now the instrument that keeps saying so.
